The Omnipresent Strahd

Posted 21 July 2026 · 416 points on r/CurseofStrahd

I’ve run Curse of Strahd campaign about five times, almost entirely with the legacy stat block and perhaps 20ish more times in "Strahd must Die" events. I haven’t run the new version at the table yet, so some of this would need to be reworked for it. R.I.P my OG.

What finally made my Strahd scary wasn’t increasing his numbers. It was making him calculated.

I treat Strahd as having access to essentially any spell he could reasonably have collected over four centuries. He has the Amber Temple, a lich’s library, and more time than anyone should. His prepared list changes with his agenda.

If he’s doing reconnaissance, he prepares for that. If he plans to scry, he prepares for that. If he expects a fight, the combat and contingency spells come out. The party should never feel like they’ve solved his permanent loadout, because he isn’t preparing for a generic adventuring day. He is preparing for them. Combined with information that he gains through scrying, sending wolves, bats, and rats to constantly check up on the party and the Vistani, he should have all the information that he needs to exploit everything that he wants to about them. When the time comes.

Inside Ravenloft, damage is almost secondary. His home is his advantage.

The castle isn’t a dungeon he lives in. It’s a weapon he’s holding. It is its own breathing organism.

Endless minions from every corner of the castle, bat and rat swarms can come from everywhere. And it just adds to the vibe. You know plenty of undead and occasionally a vampire or two. Swarms are nothing to the blaster but what it does is it applies pressure. Applying pressure makes them think, makes them use resources, and makes them slow down. Keep that in mind when you read the next bit.

His first objective is to stop the party from functioning as a party:

  1. Spread them across the castle.
  1. Lock doors between them.
  1. Use minions to drive them in different directions.
  1. Keep them separated instead of letting the minions die in one convenient battle.
  1. Take his time with whichever isolated character gives him the best advantage.
  1. Phase through the castle and use its geometry while everyone else scrambles to reconnect. Allow himself to heal a bit while moving between targets.

Once the party loses its shared action economy, he doesn’t need to stand in a room trading damage with five heroes. He appears where he wants, applies pressure, takes the available opportunity attacks, and disappears again.

Outside the finale, I use him like the Cheshire Cat with Alice. He appears often. He’s charming. Sometimes he’s almost helpful. Then, at some point, it stops being friendly.

The contrast matters. If he snarls and threatens everyone from the first meeting, there’s nowhere for him to go. Establishing his composure first makes the moments when he genuinely lashes out much more frightening.

My favorite introduction happens after Death House. The party finds a welcome basket from Strahd: food, a good bottle of Red Dragon Crush, garlic, wooden stakes, and assorted gifts for their stay in Barovia. The black carriage is just pulling away as the players come out.

It’s hospitality as a threat.

The message is: “Please, make yourself comfortable. You may even try the stakes if you feel the need.”

Across those campaigns, Strahd personally killed at least one character in every run. Sometimes the party was powerful enough to turn that into a resurrection problem rather than a permanent death. That’s fine. I don’t treat it as a scoreboard. The point is that when he finally stops smiling, the table believes him.

How often do you bring Strahd directly into contact with the party before Ravenloft, and what makes the transition from charming host to genuine threat work at your table?

Appendix: driving a wedge after the Amber Temple

You brought up a real strong narrative nuance, which is the fact that you can use Strahd to try and drive a wedge in the party. I do run this play in one particular spot of my campaign very aggressively and that's after some or any of the players have taken the dark powers at the Amber Temple. In my campaign, taking a dark power is a lose condition regardless of whatever else happens after that. The dark powers own you and they won't ever let you leave and that's the story that you get. A tragic one. I use Strahd to tease that fact. Even if you don't break the party up, it's still a powerful narrative. Just be sure to do that little bit privately with that one player and let them marinate with the information that they received.
