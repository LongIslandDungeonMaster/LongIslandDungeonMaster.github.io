One six-sided die, one sheet, and the rules of the game you already play.

What This Is

The die answers the questions the Dungeon Master used to answer. The sheet remembers what it said.

You need three things: a character you already have, one six-sided die (a d6; two are handier, because shading and the Turn use two), and the sheet on the last page of this booklet. Print it, or copy it onto one side of a page.

Play your game exactly as you always do. A locked door is a check. A fight is a fight. A spell does what the spell says. None of that changes. The die is for the one thing you cannot do alone: answer the question you would have asked the person behind the screen. Is the gate watched? Does he know my face? Did they leave a guard?

There are five parts. Learn them in this order.

The five parts

How one scene runs

Do these seven things, in this order, every scene.

  1. FRAME. Say where you are going and what you want out of it. One sentence each. The second sentence is your OBJECTIVE.
  2. CHECK. Roll the SCENE CHECK against your PRESSURE boxes (under Pressure). The scene happens as framed, or it is altered, or something interrupts it.
  3. PLAY. Use the rules of the game for everything the rules of the game cover. Set the difficulty yourself before you roll: 10 is easy, 15 is hard, 20 is very hard.
  4. ASK. For anything the rules do not cover and you do not already know, ask the die. Three questions per scene, no more.
  5. TURN. When an Ask comes up 1 or 6, roll two more dice and read the table. Do not interpret. Read it and play it. A 1 or a 6 fires a Turn only on an Ask: on the Scene Check, a name roll, a roll on a person's table, or a check against the game's rules, it never does. On a shaded Ask only the die you kept counts, and if the two dice match it is a Wrong Question instead, not a Turn.
  6. LOG. Write one line on the sheet for anything the dice established. It is now true and it stays true.
  7. PRESSURE. If anything on the fill list happened, fill one box. One box per scene at most.

When there is a fight

Use the game's combat rules. One character alone is a fair match for one creature with a challenge rating of a quarter of your level (a quarter or less at level 1; at level 5 and above, half your level, or two creatures at a quarter). Before you roll initiative, say in one sentence what winning means, and it is rarely "everything is dead". Leaving a fight is always allowed, and it counts as ending the scene without your objective.

Example. FRAME: I go to the toll house to find out why the barge is late. OBJECTIVE: leave knowing where the barge is. CHECK: one box is filled. I roll a 4, higher than one, so the scene happens as framed. PLAY: I walk in and talk to the reeve. ASK: "Does the reeve know where the barge is?" I roll a 6. TURN: two more dice, 1 and 4. The table says he tells me a fact I could not have asked for. LOG: I write it on the sheet. PRESSURE: nothing on the fill list happened. No box.

The Ask

Frame a yes-or-no question. Say what a yes means and what a no means. Roll a d6.

The ladder
d6 Answer What happens
1 No, and it gets worse. Roll a TURN AGAINST (its own section, after The Turn).
2 No. Nothing else. Play on.
3 No, but. You have advantage on your next roll in this scene, a check or an Ask. It lapses when the scene ends. This is not a shade (see below): if the two dice match, nothing happens.
4 Yes, but. Your next Ask in this scene is shaded UNLIKELY. If it was going to be LIKELY the two cancel and you roll one die. If it was already UNLIKELY it stays UNLIKELY. If there is no next Ask this scene, it does nothing.
5 Yes. Nothing else. Play on.
6 Yes, and it gets better. Roll a TURN FOR (its own section, after Turn Against).

Before you roll, do these three things

  1. Ask it as a yes-or-no question. "Is the gate watched?" is a question. "What is behind the gate?" is not, and the die cannot answer it. If you roll for it anyway the result is void and it still counts as one of your three.
  2. Say out loud what a yes means and what a no means. If you cannot say both, there is no question. Decide it yourself and play on.
  3. Do not ask what you do not want answered. A no you are going to argue with is not a question. Decide it and play on. The die has no authority you did not give it.

Shading the odds

If the answer is LIKELY, roll two dice and keep the higher. If it is UNLIKELY, roll two dice and keep the lower. A plain Ask is a yes half the time. LIKELY makes it two times in three and UNLIKELY one time in six, and it needs no chart. One shaded roll in six is neither a yes nor a no, because both dice match. There is no third shade. "Nearly certain" is not a question, it is a fact: write it on the sheet and do not roll.

Shading is the only time you roll two dice for one question. When you do, and both dice show the same face, stop: that is THE WRONG QUESTION (its own section).

Three questions per scene

Count them. The fourth question you answer yourself, and you will find you already knew. Everything counts against the three: a question about a place, a question that came back as a Wrong Question, a question with no yes or no in it, and a yes you got from a person without rolling (under The People).

Asking who and why

The die never tells you who did it or why. That is yours to decide. Decide it, then test it: "Is it the reeve?" is a question the die can answer. Shade it by what your FACTS say. If the die says no, decide again and ask again. If both dice match, the Wrong Question will tell you which part of your idea was wrong.

Example. "Does the ferryman know the two messengers?" He works the only crossing, so it is LIKELY. I roll two dice, 2 and 5, keep the 5. Yes. Next: "Will he tell me for a silver piece?" His three lines say he wants to be left alone, so it is UNLIKELY. I roll 3 and 3. Doubles. That is a Wrong Question: something I assumed is false. I do not get an answer, and I have used two of my three.

The Turn

On a 1 or a 6, roll two more dice. The first says what gets touched. The second says what happens to it.

Most oracles hand you two abstract words here and leave you to make them mean something. This one does not. The first die points at something already on your sheet. The second die says what happens to it. Read the cell where they meet and play it.

Procedure

  1. Roll a fresh die. That is the TARGET: what in your game gets touched. Find it in the table below.
  2. Roll a second fresh die. That is the OPERATION: what happens to it. Three dice in all: the Ask, then these two.
  3. Turn to the grid. If the Ask was a 1, read TURN AGAINST (its own section, after The Turn). On a 6, read TURN FOR (its own section, after Turn Against). Find the target's table, then the row for the operation.
  4. Read the cell and play it. Name the exact person, thing, clock or place it lands on. Do not soften it. Do not improve it.
  5. Cross the cell off. A used cell is spent for the rest of this story.
The first die: the target
d6 The target Which means
1 The one in front of you The person or creature in this scene. If nobody is here, the nearest sign that one was.
2 The absent name Somebody on your cast list who is not in this scene.
3 What you carry Gear, coin, supply, a prepared spell, the thing you were sent to fetch. Read it off your character sheet.
4 The place The ground, the light, the weather, the way in, the way out.
5 The count The clock, the schedule, the appointment, how long you thought you had.
6 You Your body, your name, your face, what the world knows about you.

When the cell names something that is not on your sheet

A cell may mention a favour owed, a debt nobody remembers, the person you came to find, somebody working to your schedule. The draw has just created it, and it is true from now on. Give it a name, put it on the sheet, and play on. One new thing per draw and no more. A person together with what they want from you counts as one thing.

When the target is not there

  • Target 1, nobody present: read it as the nearest sign that somebody was.
  • Target 2, no absent cast member: a new person walks in. Roll the walk-in table (under The People) for who, roll a name, and apply the operation to them. If the cell speaks of an old dealing with them, they came because of it. You did not know them; they knew of you.
  • Target 3, nothing worth naming: read it as the one thing you would least like to lose.
  • Target 5, no clock running: read it as the one deadline this scene implies, and write that deadline down as a clock before the scene ends. If there is none, roll the target again.
  • Two or more absent cast members: take the one the dice have used least.

When the cell is already crossed off

Move the second die up by one (6 goes back to 1) until you reach a cell that is not crossed off. If the whole table is gone, roll the first die again. If that table is gone too, the tables have nothing left to say about this situation: read a 1 as a plain No and a 6 as a plain Yes.

Two rules that always hold

No cell ends the scene. A cell changes what the scene is about; what you do next is yours. And no cell tells you what you feel or decide. It reports the world.

Example. I ask whether the vestry is unlocked and roll a 1. TURN AGAINST. First die 2: the absent name. Second die 3: it turns. The cell reads: "They have gone over to the other side, and everything you told them went with them." My cast has one absent name, Hett the reeve. So Hett has changed sides and knows everything I told him. I write it in FACTS, move his standing to hostile, and cross the cell off.

Turn Against

You rolled a 1. Find the target's table. Read the row for the second die.

The second die: the operation
d6 Operation In one word
1 It arrives Something enters the scene.
2 It breaks It fails, tears, floods, goes out, stops working.
3 It turns Intent or allegiance reverses.
4 It is seen You are known, marked, recorded, recognised.
5 It costs The price goes up, or it wants something first.
6 It was false Something you took as true is not.
1. The one in front of you
d6 Draw
1 Somebody they answer to walks in. Everything said from here is said to two audiences.
2 An injury or affliction they were hiding stops being hideable. Whatever they were going to do for you, they can do less of it.
3 What they wanted a minute ago is not what they want now, and the new want is the thing you came here holding.
4 They recognise you, and they say so out loud, in front of whoever else is in the room.
5 They will still do it. Not at the agreed price. The new price is something you did not bring.
6 The role is real and the name is not. Whoever this is, they are standing in for the person you came to find.
2. The absent name
d6 Draw
1 They are here, now, hours before anyone expected them, and they came by the short road.
2 Word reaches you that they are hurt, taken, or finished. Whatever they were holding for you is not being held.
3 They have gone over to the other side, and everything you told them went with them.
4 They have been talking about you to somebody who was asking questions. That somebody is the next stranger you meet, and they already know. Decide what changes before then.
5 The favour they owed you has already been spent. Somebody else got there first, and they got it cheap.
6 What you were told about them is a year out of date. The person you are counting on is not who is walking around with that name, and you have to decide what to do with the one you have got.
3. What you carry
d6 Draw
1 There is something in your pack that you did not put there. It has been there long enough to matter.
2 The one piece of gear this scene needs fails right now, in the way it fails worst.
3 Somebody present wants a specific thing you are carrying, and they knew you had it before you walked in.
4 What you carry is recognised, and carrying it here is a claim, a trespass, or a crime.
5 You can keep going, and it spends supply, coin, or a slot you were saving for the thing after this.
6 It is not what you were told it was. Look at it properly and it reads differently.
4. The place
d6 Draw
1 Somebody is coming in behind you. You hear it a full beat before you can see it.
2 The way out gives way: the floor, the bridge, the rope, the lamp, the door. Leaving is a check now.
3 This place is in use. It belongs to someone, they are due back before this scene ends, and the signs of that are everywhere once you look.
4 You left a mark here and it can be read: a print, blood, a broken seal, a door you did not close.
5 Going further takes a toll: a name spoken, a coin, a rite, or a piece of gear left at the threshold.
6 This is not the place you were told it was. The name on your map belongs to somewhere else.
5. The count
d6 Draw
1 It happens now. Not at dusk, not tomorrow. Now, while you are standing here.
2 The thing that was measuring the time stops: the bell, the tide, the watch, the candle. You go on blind.
3 The schedule has turned against somebody who was helping you. Any help from them now is hurried help.
4 Somebody else is working to the same schedule and knows exactly when you will be where. Change the schedule, or be there first.
5 You can buy time, and the price is a promise. Write a Mark: whoever sells you the hour names what it will cost, and they name it later.
6 The deadline was never the real one. It falls sooner than anyone told you, and the next time its clock would tick, it ticks twice.
6. You
d6 Draw
1 Somebody from a scene that looked finished has come looking for you, and they did not come alone.
2 The injury or the exhaustion you have been carrying stops being something you can carry. Apply it by the rules, now.
3 Something you did earlier is being told as a different story, and the story has already moved faster than you have. Correct it now, or that is the version that lasts.
4 Your name is written down somewhere official: a list, a notice, a toll ledger, a warrant.
5 You can have what you came for. It costs you the goodwill, the standing, or the protection you have been living on.
6 A promise you made was heard as a larger promise than you made, and the person holding you to it has every right.

Turn For

You rolled a 6. Find the target's table. Read the row for the second die. Then take the bonus.

The second die: the operation. Read the cell and play it, and take the bonus in the last column as well
d6 Operation In one word And you also get
1 It opens A way through that was not there. One extra Ask this scene.
2 It owes Somebody takes your side. A cast member present moves one step toward warm.
3 It holds What should have failed does not. Advantage on your next roll this scene.
4 It tells A fact you could not have asked for. Write one FACT with no speaker, about the thing the first die named.
5 It is early You have more time, or you are first. A running clock loses one box. If none is running, one extra Ask this scene.
6 It is thinner than it looks The obstacle is softer than advertised. Your next Ask this scene is shaded LIKELY.
1. The one in front of you
d6 Draw
1 They name a second way in, and they can describe it well enough to use.
2 They take your side once, out loud, in front of somebody whose opinion costs them.
3 They keep your secret, and they keep it after it stops being free to keep.
4 They give you the thing they were holding back. Answer the last question you left unasked.
5 You catch them at the door. Five more minutes and they would have been gone, and they know it, which is worth something. Use it.
6 The refusal is a position, not a wall. They want to be argued out of it, and one good reason does it.
2. The absent name
d6 Draw
1 They left you a way in: a key where you would look, a bar lifted, a name you are allowed to use.
2 Word comes that they spent their own standing to clear your path with somebody who matters. Use it now, because it is spent by morning.
3 They held the position you had already written off. It cost them, it held, and they need relieving before this scene ends.
4 A message from them, and it names the thing you have been circling for two scenes. Go there, or send word back. Either one costs the rest of this scene.
5 They arrive before the trouble does, which has never once happened before. Decide what the two of you do with the minutes before it gets here.
6 Whoever is set against you has fewer people than the rumour gave them. Halve the number.
3. What you carry
d6 Draw
1 Something you are carrying fits this lock, this mechanism, this door, and nobody ever said it would. Use it here and it is known to be yours.
2 A maker's mark on your gear means something to somebody here, and they will do one thing for whoever carries it. Ask for it now or keep it for later.
3 The thing that should have been ruined is intact. Soaked, scorched, filthy, and good for one more use. Decide when.
4 Go through it properly. There is something in there that was never on the list.
5 You have more of it than the tally said: one more dose, one more day, one more charge. Decide now who it is for.
6 Nothing in the pack is as heavy as it was packed. Set down, sell or hand over the thing slowing you down, knowing whoever gets it will know where you have been.
4. The place
d6 Draw
1 There is a second way out, and it is standing open.
2 Somebody kept this place ready for whoever came next, and you are whoever came next. Take what was left, and decide whether to leave a sign that you did.
3 The cover holds: the dark, the storm, the noise of the crowd, for exactly as long as you need it. Do the thing you would not have risked.
4 The ground reads. How many came through, which way they went, and whether they were carrying. Pick one trail and follow it before this scene ends.
5 You are here ahead of whoever was supposed to be here first. Nobody has taken a position yet, so choose the ground.
6 The defence is for show. No one is at the post, and no one has been for a while. Walk through it, or wait and see who was meant to be standing there.
5. The count
d6 Draw
1 Something goes wrong elsewhere and it buys you a full extra scene before the deadline.
2 Somebody stalls it on your behalf and takes the consequence of stalling it themselves.
3 The thing that looked ready to come apart on a schedule holds one more day.
4 You learn exactly when it falls. An hour, not a guess. Write it down as a fact and frame the scene that comes just before it.
5 You are early enough that the other side is still assembling. Nobody is in place, so choose the ground.
6 Missing the deadline costs money or standing, not blood. It was never the disaster you were told, so decide whether to pay it.
6. You
d6 Draw
1 A door opens on who you are: your trade, your face, your accent, the name you were trying not to use. Walk through it or deny it. Both are remembered.
2 Somebody you never counted on steps up for you. Name them from an earlier scene, not a new one.
3 Your cover holds under a direct question from somebody who had every reason to press.
4 Something you wrote down earlier and never used is the thing that matters here. Go and read it. If you wrote nothing, it is the most recent fact you learned.
5 You move first. In a fight, advantage on initiative or a surprise round if you were already positioned. Out of one, you act before whoever was about to, and they know it.
6 It was never aimed at you. You are not the target, you never were, and whoever is does not know that yet. Decide whether to tell them.

The Wrong Question

You shaded a question and both dice showed the same face. The die does not answer. Something you assumed is false.

Every question has an assumption inside it. "Will the sexton open the vestry for me?" assumes there is a vestry, that it has a door, that he has the key, and that he is the sexton. On doubles, one of those is wrong.

Procedure

  1. Do not read the roll as an answer. The question is void. It still counts as one of your three.
  2. Say the assumptions out loud. There are always several.
  3. Roll a d6 on the table below. It tells you which kind of assumption was wrong.
  4. Correct it with the smallest change that fits everything already on your sheet. Not the most dramatic. The smallest. This is what keeps every session from turning into a twist ending.
  5. Write the correction in FACTS. Then ask a different question, or act.
Which assumption was wrong
d6 What was wrong It sounds like
1 Identity. The one you have been calling the steward has never been the steward.
2 Access. There is no door. There has not been a door since the flood. That is a curtain.
3 Time. It already happened. It happened while you were on the road.
4 Capability. He cannot read. Every written order you watched him take was read to him by somebody else.
5 Allegiance. They are not holding it against the thing outside. They are holding it shut for the thing outside.
6 Condition. The cargo is not late. There was never any cargo.

Example. "Will the ferryman tell me for a silver piece?" UNLIKELY, two dice, 3 and 3. Doubles. Assumptions: that he can be paid, that he knows, that he is the ferryman, that the crossing is his. I roll a 4: Capability. The smallest correction that fits my sheet: he does not know. He was not on the water that night; his boy was. I write "the ferryman's boy took the crossing that night" in FACTS. The boy is now a name on my cast.

Pressure

Six boxes on the sheet. They fill as things go wrong. They never empty. When the sixth fills, the story ends.

Fill one box at the end of a scene if any of these happened

  • You rolled a TURN AGAINST, including one the Scene Check forced.
  • You ended the scene without your OBJECTIVE.
  • A day passed: you slept the night, or spent a full day on the road. Once per day, not once per rest.
  • You were SEEN: somebody learned your name, your face, or your business, and you did not offer it. Somebody means anyone on your cast, or anyone who could carry it to them. Introducing yourself is not being seen.

Three rules

  1. One box per scene, no matter how many of those happened. Two things going wrong in one scene is one box.
  2. Boxes never empty. There is no way to take one back. Finishing things does not calm the world; it decides what the ending is about (under The Ending).
  3. A scene where nothing on the list happened fills nothing. You can play a careful scene and the boxes stay where they are.

Played this way the sixth box fills between the sixth scene and the tenth, most often at the seventh or eighth. That is one sitting, and an ending at the end of it.

What the filled boxes do to the ladder
Boxes filled What changes
0 to 2 The ladder reads as printed.
3 to 4 A 3 reads as a plain No. No more advantage from it.
5 A 3 reads as a plain No, and a 5 reads as Yes, but. Everything costs.
6 The story ends. Turn to The Ending. The ladder keeps reading as it did at five.

Example. Scene 4: I rolled a 1 (a Turn Against) and I was recognised (seen) and I left without the toll book (no objective). Three things on the list. I fill one box. Boxes: three. From now on a 3 is a plain No.

The Scene Check

Do this at the start of every scene, after you have written the frame and the objective.

  1. Roll a d6. If it is higher than the number of filled boxes, the scene happens as framed. Play on.
  2. If it is equal to or lower, the scene is not what you expected. Roll a d6 again.
  3. On 1 to 3, ALTERED: the scene happens, but roll a TURN AGAINST first and apply it to the setup before your first move.
  4. On 4 to 6, INTERRUPTED: roll a d6 and read that numbered thread slot on your sheet. If the slot holds a thread, that is what interrupts you: roll a TURN AGAINST and land it on that thread. The scene you framed does not happen yet. If the slot is empty, nothing pulls: treat it as ALTERED.

With no boxes filled the check cannot fire. With five filled, it fires five times in six. Do not roll it during the ending.

Example. Four boxes filled. I frame a scene at the mill and roll the check: 2. That is not higher than four, so it fires. Second roll: 5, INTERRUPTED. I roll for the slot: 3. Slot 3 holds "Who is the tallyman waiting for?" I roll a TURN AGAINST and land it on the tallyman. The mill scene waits.

The People

Anybody you want something from gets three lines. Each line is a decision, not an adjective.

Personality tables give you words like "gruff" and "greedy". You cannot negotiate with an adjective. Give a person three lines instead:

  • WANTS: the thing they are trying to get, stated as something you could offer, take, or reach first.
  • WON'T: the one thing they will not do, whatever you offer. This is the wall.
  • TURNS ON: the one thing that would move them off the wall. It is usually information.

Write them yourself when you know the person. Roll them when you do not. Roll only when you want something from them; a stranger you ask one question of needs a name and nothing more.

The three lines
d6 They want They will not They turn on
1 To be paid what they are owed Cross one specific person Proof, written or witnessed, that bears on what they want
2 Somebody back Go to one specific place Their name cleared of one thing
3 To be left alone Be seen helping you Somebody else going first
4 To be told it was not their fault Break a rule they keep for themselves The one they fear, removed
5 The thing you are carrying Say the part that incriminates them A debt honoured that nobody remembers
6 To matter here Move before somebody else does Being asked properly, by somebody who knows a fact about them

Every person also has a STANDING with you: hostile, wary, or warm. Most people start wary. It moves one step at a time: on something you did in play, or when a Turn says so. A check never moves it. A hostile person shades every Ask about their help UNLIKELY. A warm one shades it LIKELY. When their standing and one of their three lines point different ways, the line wins.

Talking to someone: the procedure

  1. Say what you want from them, in one sentence. "I want him to leave the gate unbarred tonight."
  2. Check it against their three lines. Touches their WANTS: the Ask is LIKELY. Runs into their WON'T: UNLIKELY. Neither: roll one die.
  3. If their TURNS ON was satisfied in an earlier scene, do not roll. They say yes. Cross that line out; it never works again, and roll a new WANTS for them. The yes still counts as one of your three Asks. Satisfied in this same scene: roll, and the Ask is LIKELY.
  4. Roll the Ask. The die says whether they agree. You say how.
  5. If the answer is no and you want the scene to go on, roll on the table below.
What they do instead of yes
d6 Instead, they Which gives you
1 Send you to somebody else who can, or who they say can. A new name for your cast, and possibly a lie.
2 Name a price, more than it is worth to them. A decision, and a Mark if you pay it in public.
3 Say not now. Come back at a set time, or after a set thing. A clock, or a scene you now have to make happen.
4 Refuse, and tell you why in a way that costs them nothing. A FACT. Write it down.
5 Offer a lesser thing. Not what you asked for. Adjacent and real. A choice.
6 Go and fetch whoever decides. The scene escalates. If that person matters, you have been seen.

Lying

The die tells you what they say. It never tells you whether it is true. Write what a person told you in FACTS with their name in brackets after it: the wagons crossed at dawn (Nan's word). A line with a speaker can be wrong. A line with no speaker was established by the dice and cannot be. If somebody's statement contradicts a line with no speaker, they are lying, and you do not need to roll to know it.

Naming someone

Roll a d6 and say the first thing that fits. 1 a trade: the tallyman, the salter. 2 a place: the one from the hill farm. 3 kin: the widow's boy. 4 a mark on them: the burned hand, the good coat. 5 a rank: captain, sister, your honour. 6 what they are called behind their back. The first name out of your mouth is the name. Write it down.

When somebody has to walk in and you have nobody

Roll a d6. 1 the one you wronged. 2 the one who wants the same thing; they will deal before they fight. 3 the law: a watch, a reeve, a temple, a landlord, a gang. 4 the scavenger, who follows trouble and has followed you. 5 the messenger, sent by a name on your sheet, or by whoever sent you here. 6 the one who should not be here. Name them and put them on the cast. They never walk in as a stranger again.

Example. The reeve. WANTS: the toll book back. WON'T: cross the tallyman. TURNS ON: proof in writing. I want him to open the toll house for me. That touches his want, so LIKELY: I roll 1 and 4, keep the 4. Yes, but. He opens it, and my next Ask this scene is UNLIKELY. Later I find a written order in the tallyman's hand and show it to him: that is his TURNS ON, satisfied. Next scene I ask him to arrest the tallyman and do not roll. He says yes. I cross the line out.

The Ending

The sixth box is filled. Stop opening new doors. Build the last scenes from what is already on the sheet.

Most solo games do not end. They stop, with eleven questions open and nobody to answer them. This one ends, and the ending is made of three things you already wrote down.

Procedure, done once

  1. Name THE THREE. The first is whatever you were facing when the sixth box filled: the person, the place, or the thing in the scene. Fill the other two from the sheet, in this order, taking the first that exists: open threads at weight 2 or more, heaviest first; then Marks, oldest first; then a clock with at least one box filled; then the cast member whose standing with you is worst; then a thread at weight 1. If two of the three turn out to be the same person, the ending is about that person, and you play the shape with two.
  2. Close what you can answer. A thread whose question you can now answer in one sentence is closed, not counted. Write the answer.
  3. Do not invent anything. No new threads, no new people. A thin sheet gets a thin ending. That is the honest result.
  4. Roll the SHAPE on the table below. It says how the three collide.
  5. Name the place and the hour yourself. The dice have no opinion.
  6. Stop rolling the Scene Check. Keep framing scenes and keep to three Asks in each. The ladder reads as it did at five boxes. The Turn still fires on a 1 or a 6.
  7. Play to the end. Each of the three gets one resolution line on the sheet, including the ones that end badly. A Mark resolves by being paid, denied, or made true in front of everyone. A thread the story has moved past is written off as cold, with one line of reason.
How the three collide
d6 The shape How to run it
1 The siege Everything converges on one place and you are inside it. The three are three ways in, and you cannot hold all of them.
2 The chase What you want is leaving. Each of the three is a stage of the pursuit, in the order you wrote them. Whoever you neglected is waiting at the last one.
3 The trial You answer for it in front of people who can decide. Each of the three is evidence, and whoever holds a mark on you is the prosecution.
4 The rite Somebody is doing a thing on a schedule and it finishes tonight. Each of the three is a piece you can remove, and removing one changes the outcome rather than stopping it.
5 The turned friend The heaviest of the three runs through the person on your cast who thinks best of you. The other two are the reason and the price.
6 The debt called in Everything you borrowed comes due at once. The three are three creditors and they have all arrived on the same day.

Example. The sixth box fills while I am standing in the tallyman's yard with the tallyman. He is the first of the three. My heaviest open thread is "Who is the tallyman waiting for?" at weight 3: the second. No other thread is above weight 1, so the third is my oldest Mark: my name in the reeve's hold-list. Shape: I roll a 3, the trial. So: the tallyman, the man he was waiting for, and the hold-list with my name on it, in front of people who can decide. I name the place, the toll house, and the hour, dawn. I play it out and write three lines.

The next story

When the last line is written: cross off every closed thread and every thread that names nothing. Keep your cast, your marks, your facts, and any clock still running. Uncross every cell in both Turn grids. Empty the six boxes. Start the next story on the same sheet. The world remembers everything, and what you left unpaid last time is what the sixth target reaches for first.

The Sheet

One side of one page. The dice read from it, so keep it.

The last page of this booklet is the sheet. Print it. This is what each block on it is for. Your gear stays on your character sheet; the third target reads from there.

Cast

One line per person, the moment they get a name: name, standing (hostile, wary, warm), and the three lines if you need them. Five names by the end of your first session is about right. The second target reaches for them constantly.

Threads

Six numbered slots. Each holds one open question, written as a question, with a WEIGHT from 1 to 5 and a note of what it names: a person, a place, a clock, a mark, or a thing you carry. A question that names nothing is not a thread and takes no slot. "What is going on here?" is not a thread. "What does the ferryman know about the missing messengers?" is.

Weight starts at 1. Add 1 when the Scene Check's interrupt roll lands on the slot, and add 1 when a Turn (against or for) lands on something the question names. Nothing else moves it. Weight never goes down. Do not name a thread after everything on the board; a thread names the one or two things it is about.

A thread closes when you can write its answer in one sentence, or when you decide it has gone cold. Write the answer beside it. Closing does not touch PRESSURE; it means the thread cannot be one of THE THREE. A closed slot stays empty and keeps its number. Never renumber. Six is a hard cap: a seventh question means you are collecting, not playing.

A thread you closed on somebody's word comes back if the word proves false: same slot if it is free, one weight heavier. Once.

Clocks

Up to three. Each has a name, four or six boxes, and two things written when you make it: what fills a box, and what happens when the last box fills. A clock with no stated trigger never ticks. A clock whose consequence can no longer happen is crossed off, and the fifth target reads the next deadline the scene implies.

Facts

What the dice established, one flat line each. If it is here, you never roll for it again. If a later result contradicts a fact, the fact wins and the result is read as the nearest thing that does not. What a person told you goes here too, with their name in brackets. A line with a speaker can be wrong.

Marks

What the world knows about you: one line for what, and who knows it. The sixth target reads from here. A Mark is erased by fixing it in the fiction, never by time.

To start a story

  1. Write one cast member with three lines. Somebody who wants something from you.
  2. Write two threads that name things. One you care about and one you do not. If the cast member's lines handed you a question, that is one of the two.
  3. Write one clock, with its trigger and its consequence.
  4. Fill the first PRESSURE box. Something has already gone wrong; that is why you are here. Then frame your first scene.

That is four minutes. Any solo game that needs an evening of preparation has misunderstood who is playing it.

To stop for the night, write the one sentence that will remind you what you were about to do. To resume, read FACTS first, then THREADS, then play.

A Night to Try: The Bell at Harrow Ford

For a character of level 1 to 3. One evening, set up for you. Thirty-five more like it are in Thirty-Six Nights.

Copy the people, the two threads and the clock onto your sheet. Fill the first Pressure box. Frame your first scene from the last sentence below. On a 1 or a 6, roll one die and read this page's table instead of the grids, and cross the row off; when a table is used up, go back to the grids. On matching dice, roll one die on the last table. The rows on the 6 table carry the same bonus as the grids, by row number.

Your uncle sent you to Harrow Ford to collect a debt from Thurstan the miller. The mill wheel is stopped, the chapel bell rang at midnight, and nobody will say who rang it. Thurstan has not been seen for three days, and his wife met you at the door.

THE PERSONRhoswen, the miller's wife, wary. WANTS her husband back, without the village learning why he left. WON'T let anyone into the mill loft. TURNS ON proof that Thurstan is alive.
ALSOThurstan, the miller, not seen for three days. WANTS the debt note back before anyone reads it. WON'T come out of hiding while the reeve is at the ford. TURNS ON being told what Rhoswen signed.
THREAD 1Where is Thurstan the miller? (Thurstan, the mill)
THREAD 2Who rang the chapel bell at midnight, and why? (the chapel bell)
THE CLOCKTHE DEBT COMES DUE, 4 boxes. Fills a box: a night passes. Full: your uncle's man rides in to collect the debt from you instead.
d6 If the die shows a 1
1 Rhoswen's brother Merrick walks in. He is the reeve, and he asks what your business is with the mill.
2 The sluice gate gives way. The mill pond drains, and whatever was sunk in it is lying in the mud for anyone to see.
3 Rhoswen no longer wants Thurstan found. She wants you out of Harrow Ford before he is, and she says so.
4 Somebody saw you at the loft door. By morning the whole ford knows a stranger was in the mill.
5 The priest will open the bell tower for you. The price is your name in the parish book as a witness, in ink.

d6 If the die shows a 6
1 The loft door is unlatched from inside. Whoever went up did not come down the stair.
2 Merrick the reeve takes your side in front of the priest, and the priest backs down.
3 The sluice holds when it should have gone. The pond keeps whatever is in it for one more night.
4 The parish book shows the bell was rung by the same hand that signed the debt note. Write it down.
5 Your uncle's man is a day late on the road. THE DEBT COMES DUE loses a box.
6 The loft lock is rusted through. One shove opens it.
d6 If both dice match: what was wrong
1 The woman at the door is not the miller's wife. She is his sister.
2 The loft has no door on the stair. It is reached from the wheel house, by the ladder.
3 Thurstan left a month ago, not three days. The village has been keeping the story short.
4 The bell cannot be rung from the tower. The rope is cut. It was rung from below.
5 Rhoswen is not hiding him from the village. She is hiding him from you.
6 The mill is not stopped. It runs at night, and it is not grinding grain.
