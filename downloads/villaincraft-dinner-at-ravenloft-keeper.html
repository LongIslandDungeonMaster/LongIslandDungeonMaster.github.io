VillainCraft: Dinner at Castle Ravenloft

VillainCraft, Volume One: Barovia
24 pages · 2 tables · 0 monsters · 0 NPCs

Run It

Read Aloud

"I understand you did some pruning at my winery. Bad weeds, every one of them." He pours, and he takes his time doing it. "The valley will be glad to have the wine flowing again. My own stores were getting light."

That's the third course. He has known about the winery for weeks. He waited until they had eaten to bring it up.

I said in my last post that the dinner deserves its own writeup, so here it is. I dooo love Dinner Night. :3

IF you can arrange to eat and actually have dinner during this part, do it! Even if you're remote, have everyone try and at least get a wine goblet/cup and something to eat. We all eat during the games anyway. Use it narratively here.

Same caveats as before: five+ campaigns, a couple hundred hours of Strahd Must Die Tonight events, legacy stat block, YMMV.

WHAT'S IN HERE. It's long. It's a guide, not a story. I am verbose. I'm trying to teach narrative technique by using examples of what worked for me. This is the complete version, both halves in one piece.

1. THE PART I'LL ARGUE A.K.A narrative leverage

They need to go to the dinner. It's one of the module's absolute high spots if played right and the book doesn't do it justice. Barely takes a swing at it, imho.

You don't force them. You make refusing expensive. The world leans on them, and they see the writing themselves and walk to the carriage on their own feet. Every party I've run this on chose to go. That's not a railroad. It's kind of like a bill coming due.

If you think that's a distinction without a difference, the two stories in the next section are the whole argument, and I'd rather have that fight in the comments than dodge it. :D

And to be clear about what the dinner actually IS, because I think a lot of DMs run it as a set piece where Strahd monologues at the table for an hour. Not me. It's a probe and a test. He's gathering intel on them all night, and the castle itself is part of the interview. This is the first time the players really figure out that he KNOWS things about them, so telegraph the hell out of it. The castle will also remember what the players do. They'll think they have scouted some things, but Strahd's going to drop some treats for them along the way that he thinks they might take based on where they've explored before. I've also heard the argument that his mother is kind of the embodiment of the castle and vice versa and the castle will protect him like a mother. The castle is one of the most absurdly overpowered parts of Strahd's kit.

If you read the loadout post, this is the Social loadout, with a twist. He's home, so he throws away the escapes. No dimension door, no second exit, no nightmare on standby. The castle is the exit. The special sauce for tonight: detect thoughts.

2. THE INVITATION A.K.A the bill coming due

When does it fire? This one really is DM fiat. I've seen DMs send it right after Death House and leave it as an open invitation. That's fine.

Me, I like it after they've been tossed around a bit, as a little insult to injury. Or right when they're starting to realize that Strahd has been scrying on them, stealing from them, studying them for a while now. And then comes the invitation. (levels 3-5 feels good for their first visit)

The timing should leave your players creeped out, and in general they'll put it off for a while. I have never once seen a party go straight away. That's fine. That's the point. If you've been setting the omnipresent-Strahd scene properly, the invitation just gnaws away at the edges of them until they deal with it. It should hang over their heads like the Sword of Damocles.

By dinner he should already understand them quite a bit, because the whole night runs on him knowing things he shouldn't. Big Brother vibes here. We double down with detect thoughts.

For the book people, the module gives you four trigger points: the magic mirror (p108), preventing St. Andral's Feast (p124), Ireena reaching Sergei (p156), and Ezmerelda's failed run at him (p172). Use them or don't. What matters is that it's triggered by the players doing something that calls for his attention. Otherwise there'd be no reason for him to waste his time on them. Your Strahd should be paying attention.

Who delivers it is technically irrelevant, and that's the actual advice: route it through whoever THIS party has the strongest reaction to. If they love the three wives, it comes through them. If they've had good back-and-forths with Strahd himself, he delivers it personally. If they've watched Rahadin do something terrible on Strahd's behalf (which I highly recommend, read his story, he is a ruthless, ruthless man and a broken dog), then the letter arriving in HIS hand does the work because the players hate or love to hate him. Whatever stirs the reaction. There is a good argument to be made that Strahd will never deliver his invitation by hand.

One thing the invitation should say, in writing, in his hand: they are guaranteed safe passage to the castle and home again. Say it plainly and mean it, because he does. He is a man who keeps his word, and this is the night you establish that, at some length, in front of witnesses. Everything he gets to do to them later runs on the credit he banks right here. Plus, we get to wallop them almost immediately thereafter.

However it arrives, I almost always have them picked up in the black carriage, with the mongrelfolk driver Cyrus at the reins. Hold that thought.

3. WHEN THEY SAY NO A.K.A the price goes up in public

Yes, I've had parties refuse and delay HARD. I let one group dodge it over and over for a big chunk of the campaign. So I calculated out what a "difficult" encounter would be for them, built it entirely out of skeletons and zombies (low tier trash), surrounded their camp with it overnight, and left the black carriage sitting there outside the horde. The undead parted to let them get on.

To be clear, the swarm was handleable at their level. It probably wasn't even lethal. :3 They could have turned on spirit guardians and fireballed their way out of it. The imagery alone was enough. The players went "okay, we're just going to get this over with now, before this escalates more."

Another group got picked up by Sanguine, my reskinned vrock from the pass. A giant bat that had been borderline harassing them through the whole early campaign. Cyrus told them it was there to collect them, and they hopped right on. Because they wanted to ride the giant bat. Of all things. Sold on a giant bat ride. The plan if they refused was to grab one or two characters and fly them back to the castle so everyone else had to attend anyway. Just dangling up there in the sky.

(Yes I would split the party. My DM did it to me and I will do it to them. I still practice the "old ways".)

Didn't need it. They knew the bat wasn't going to hurt them this time, and honestly I think that's what pushed it through. The best part is I did get to do all of that but in the past. It could have happened a lot sooner but it was a far bigger deal in the past when the bat was there to specifically destroy them.

That's what "make refusing expensive" looks like in practice. They still had every option. The price of the next refusal just kept going up, in public, where they could see it. We're not ticking off hash marks behind our screen on an "anger" counter. We're gently putting our thumb on the scale in a way that feels believable to the villain. I just want to reiterate here that we're not trying to be adversarial as a DM. Narrative leverage.

4. THE CARRIAGE A.K.A Cyrus's captive audience

Nobody talks about the carriage ride and it's one of my favorite bits of the whole night.

The players ride inside so they can talk amongst themselves. Cyrus drives, and there's a little shutter that opens so he can talk to them. Every time they pass a landmark he describes it, says a couple of things about it, offers up a joke, and then rides quietly until the next thing worth pointing out.

The jokes are terrible on purpose.

"What did the vampire order at the bar? A Bloody Mary."

"Why does nobody in Barovia complain about the weather? Because he might hear you."

That tier. I keep a whole list. The point of Cyrus is contrast. He's comic relief placed specifically to put their guard down, because once they get to the dinner it should feel VERY different from that weird, goofy carriage ride. It's been pretty relaxed on the way up to this point and that's the whole tension trick here.

Two things about him that are free and canon and that I'd use even if I hated jokes. He is Strahd's own servant, not hired help, and he has served the master for uncounted years. And he wears a wooden pendant with a varnished human eyeball in it, a hag eye, given to him by Morgantha so she can watch Strahd. Cyrus doesn't know it's magical.

So at a dinner where every single thing is surveillance, the funny little man driving the carriage is bugged, by a hag, and has no idea. That alone is just a great bit of story there so do with that what you will.

And when they roll into the courtyard (sporadic lightning, the whole show), Cyrus pulls into the carriage house and tells his last couple of jokes at a real whisper. He's trying to hide it now, because of where he is. He just can't help himself. Remember that. It comes back at the dinner table.

One note: I don't put the wives in the carriage with the players. They can deliver the letter. They don't ride along. Leave that space for the players to speculate, to plan and to plot. I love listening to the chatter on the way there, where they pass the landmarks and hear a couple bad jokes. It's always chaotic. It's a good time to prime the RP vibe before the dinner. It's light and the jokes set the bar low.

5. THE DOOR A.K.A the most annoying dinner guest :D

Rahadin greets them. Unless Ireena isn't with the party. Then he isn't at the dinner at all, because he's out watching her. Possibly going to take her during it. More on that in a minute...

Book note: as written, Rahadin walks them to the dining hall, points them in, pulls the doors shut and leaves. I sit him down at the table. It makes perfect canonical sense that he'd be there, and here's why it's worth doing mechanically.

Deathly Choir. Anyone within 10 feet of him hears the screams of the thousands of people he has killed, all the time, no roll involved. Then, as a bonus action, he can make everyone who can hear them roll a DC 16 Wisdom save for 3d10 psychic, half on a success.

Read that again with a dinner table in mind. The 10 feet is your seating chart. Whoever you sit near him is who's in it, and the damage only happens when Rahadin decides it does, which the players cannot know. So, most of the night you just play the choir. Whispering, screaming, sitting under the conversation, doing nothing. Narrate it. Is this mean? Maybe. Is it funny? Absolutely.

Then, when the table gets sassy, or when you catch somebody in a lie, it takes its toll and you call for the saves. Just for a little bit. And then Strahd has Rahadin excuse himself, or moves him to the other end of the table. A damage aura running underneath polite dinner conversation. It is such an interesting mechanic to play with in a social setting and I almost never see anybody use it. It's been my secret for a little bit that I'm giving up now. Hope it mortifies your players like it does mine.

Fire it rarely. Half damage on a success means it always hurts, and 3d10 into a level 6 wizard between courses is a real hit, not a "mood". Seat him near a mix, some who'll shrug off a DC 16 and some who won't.

Also: nobody takes their weapons at the door. Disarming guests would imply they're dangerous. (I do disarm them at the wedding, but that's a whole other post.) The players shouldn't be "OP" when they get here yet. But potentially some of their builds are starting to come online. We can deal with the party.

Also also, while you're describing the entrance: spend real time on the dragon statues and point out the gargoyles. Trust me. Foreshadowing pays off later if you run the wedding. :3

6. THE GUEST ROOM A.K.A the staff are the hooks

Before anybody sits down, they get shown upstairs to dress. Clothes are laid out. Their sizes. Nobody measured them. Add something for each PC that is unique to that PC. Fresh cut flowers from the valley that PC is from, rare to get anywhere else.

Do not skip this and do not rush it. It's the only stretch of the night where the party is alone in his house with time to talk, and it's the single best window you'll get to walk NPCs past them. Have fun here.

What they do with the clothes tells you things. The one who puts it all on is playing along. The one who refuses on principle is likely going to be a problem at the table, which is useful to know before the table. The one who checks the wardrobe/windows/etc for stuff is the one who'll likely go sneaking deep into the castle later.

And the staff are the hooks. Every servant who comes through that door should be an unpaid debt:

  • A face from an abandoned quest. Gertruda is the obvious one. If your party heard about the girl who ran off to the castle and then got busy with other things, have her carry in the towels. She's fine. She's happy. She's been well treated, she'll tell them so, and she does not want to be rescued. Watch what that does to them. We should have set this quest up pretty well when they got to the village of Barovia. She should have already been on their radar.
  • Escher, and he's the most useful NPC for the PC's. He's Strahd's current consort and he knows perfectly well that he's being aged out. He'll commiserate. He'll be a little too honest. He is the only person in that castle with a reason to want the party to succeed, and he doesn't quite know it yet. If the players figure this out, they have a really good connect for some counter intel from straight inside the castle.
  • A servant playing helpless. Timid, apologetic, whispering that they should leave while they can. I use a maid for this. The party might try to save her, and that's the point, because she is going to be waiting for them somewhere in the castle later on and she will not be timid then. This is part of the test that Strahd has for the players. Allow them the chance to notice she is a vampire beforehand. Either way this story goes, it's an interesting narrative for us.
  • And if Doru got out of that church basement, he's on the staff. He serves exactly one course. He doesn't speak, he doesn't meet anybody's eye, and he leaves. If your party let him out, or told Donavich they'd handle it and then didn't, parade it past them and Strahd will not comment on it once. He doesn't have to. If you ran that scene, they will know :D
  • Honorable mention for a reanimated Ismark. If you took Ireena's brother off the board already, this is a good chance to parade him around a bit. He doesn't need to have a story per se but using him here and again at the wedding is just a great way to rub your players the wrong way with Strahd.

7. THE TABLE A.K.A POPCORN

Here's the mechanical trick that steadied the whole scene for me. Run the dinner on initiative. Popcorn, rolled, whatever you like. Each player gets one turn before anyone gets a second. I kind of like the popcorn initiative because it keeps this "only social" initiative. Which separates it out from the standard D&D combat initiative. It also gives me an excuse to ignore the six seconds per turn thing. Because that doesn't work in a "social encounter". :D otherwise the whole dinner would be done, and all the courses would be eaten, in under 60 seconds RAW. We're just borrowing the structure here. Suspending the rules.

All of the talking between courses as well lends to an actual RP dinner for groups that are all at the table that like to actually have food and dress up the table and all that. This slides right on top and allows you to do each of these rounds of questioning around all the courses that are generally common for these cosplay dinners. This is why Popcorn Initiative works here because you don't need any die either, just whoever is ready at that point. Bingo bongo.

Why? Because Strahd is going to work each of them individually, and without structure your loudest player eats the entire hour while your quiet ones get nothing and watch. It's spotlight management and it's the same job you're already doing in combat. We're just gonna apply it narratively here.

On each turn the conversation moves. Strahd makes a statement or asks a question, the player responds, and they get to ask something back. And they'll get real answers. Potentially. If they ask the right ones. It makes the dinner just as much a narrative encounter as any fight, and it guarantees every single character gets their own "how does he know that" moment. Which is the entire point of the evening. You should prepare these questions for the players ahead of time. Just recall that we have detect thoughts running already for dinner. We are just in a prime spot right now.

Credit where it's due: u/DragnaCarta's social encounter statblock is for this exact scene. He gives you the conversation as a stat block. I'm giving you the turn order and what you can use to fill the turns. You can use both to flesh out the dinner quite a bit. The book leaves you wanting...

One staging note before the turns start. He is THERE. Not a projection, not a voice, not the illusion still running in the corner. And he is not at the organ. I know the organ is atmospheric and I know you want to use it. Don't. A man playing music for you is performing. A man sitting at the head of his own table, watching you come in, already seated, is a "threat". Have the organ play itself. This is purely my opinion.

In other runs I've done the illusion scene with him playing at the organ and had him come in physically later. This is really more of a matter of taste here. I prefer to have the real physical Strahd there from the beginning because he's not scared of them. Eventually we use that against Strahd because he is still going to be not afraid of them when he should be because of hubris.

What fills the turns is whatever they've actually done so far. I keep a running list of everything that got Strahd's attention: the hags at Bonegrinder, Vallakovich, hiding Ireena, Lady Wachter, the Abbot, the revolt in Vallaki, Baba Lysaga, any artifact they've picked up. None of it is scripted. Every line should make them feel like he knows a bit too much though. This is unfortunately going to be part of your prep and it's generally fun because you can aggregate all of the information that you got from spies/scrying/Vasili and make some targeted lines for each of your PCs.

Some of mine, close-ish to how I say them. You should re-sculpt these around the events that you have in your campaign. These are just some examples.

The winery. "I understand you did some pruning at my winery. Bad weeds, every one of them. The valley will be glad to have the wine flowing again. My own stores were getting light." (Then he opens something special to commemorate the occasion.)

Izek. "You'll forgive me if I don't mourn Izek. Though I would hold off on the headstone. The Abbot has been very generous lately."

Berez. "You've been to Berez. Tell me, how did you find my dear old grandmother? She was always so fond of visitors."

A party quick to violence. "You leave a great many bodies behind you, for people so certain they're the heroes of this."

A party that lied and cheated its way through something. "If you're not cheating, are you really trying?"

Now, what is he actually doing for the next hour. Three things, at once:

  • Isolating them. He works one person at a time on purpose, and he says things to individuals that the rest of the table can hear. A compliment aimed at one. A gift offered to one. An old secret about one, produced in front of everybody. He'll barter, too, and it does not have to be a real offer to work: that item you're carrying, for the name of somebody who's been helping you. The point isn't the trade. The point is that four people just watched a fifth get offered something.
  • Auditioning them. He is looking for the ones with the "darkest heart", and he is genuinely asking whether that person could hold Barovia. More on that at the bottom, because the actual offer never happens at this table.
  • Rewriting the history. He'll tell them the clean version of his own story, the "liberator version", the one where the valley was saved and the ingratitude came later. He wants to see who nods, who goes quiet, and who has been reading the Tome and now knows he's lying. Whoever can't keep their face still just told him what they've found. Part of that abuser control vibe.

What I'd most want you to steal out of this whole section -> He asks them about themselves. Where are you from? What did you do before this? Who taught you that. Do you have family. Ordinary dinner-party questions, the same ones a real host asks, and your players will answer them because it's polite and because they like talking about their characters. In reality you're doing backstory work with your characters. Something, something, two birds.

Ask real questions. Get real answers. We are mining them actively for our "Shades of Grey" endings later. This is how little bits of DM magic are made because they're gonna have mentioned this stuff in passing. God knows how long before this campaign ended and you hit them with this in the epilogue. :3

The cautionary tale, from me to you: too much talking during dinner and it'll be reallly boring. I have done it. I have seen it. I have lived through it. Don't do it. :D

Keep the courses coming, keep the turns moving (unless you are eating then by all rights take as long as you need). BUT I can't stress as a paid DM one of my most used skills is editing to make sure that the pacing of the game is appropriate. He is charming, he is curious, he asks about their stay, he gives them a few genuinely useful tips, and he lets them get comfortable BEFORE he starts bringing up the things they've done here.

There are also five bigger "set-pieces" depending on where your campaign is: Marina, the vow of vengeance, the "go liberate Vallaki for me" quest, the Patrina story if there's an elf at the table, and the successor reveal if the party looks fractured. Elven Tower covers all five in more depth than I'm going to here, and some of my notes on them started as his back in the day.

Strahd hands out work, and this is underused. He is the most powerful being in the valley and he has jobs he'd rather other people did. Find Van Richten for him. Tell him where the Keepers are nesting. Bring Ireena somewhere she'll be safe. Every one of those is a real quest with a real reward, and every one of them is him getting the party to do his intelligence work for free. The good ones will smell it. Some will do it anyway. This is part of probing for a successor, we are going to dangle these out and see if anyone bites. And we can make it enticing. Strahd has resources.

(Using Charm on players at the dinner table is really a table-safety question and not a Strahd one. Short version: charm an NPC they like instead, and if you must charm a PC during dinner, ask for something small and awful, not something violent.) Have them swap chairs, or move closer to him, or pour wine for someone else, like one of his wives.

8. THE FOOD A.K.A the red herring

The meal is lavish and it is real. Most of it is imported from outside the mists by the Vistani, specifically for this. Fresh fruit in a country that starves. Don't explain it. It tells them something without anybody saying it: Strahd controls the door in and out of this place, and he can reach through it whenever he likes.

The wine is offered as a choice. Red Dragon Crush (the good one, and he'll say so), Champagne du le Stomp, Purple Grapemash No. 3. And a fourth bottle with a label that someone at the table can read in another language. Drow Elf Mushroom Wine is what I like to use here. Because it can be, regardless of where the players are from, a great distance to get to the Underdark from anywhere, it serves the point. We're just framing out that the mist is passable. This is poured last, for whoever he's most interested in so far. This means he could just let anyone through as he pleases, if he feels like it.

Now the fun part. The vampires at the table drink only the "wine," and if asked, they insist they'll have their own bite to eat later. Say it exactly that flat and let it hang. Am I dinner? "I'm going to have you over for dinner." Yeah. Your dinner. Twilight Zone, To Serve Man. Let it sit each time like a mexican standoff. In my games the players kind of come in and it's Strahd, Escher, and the wives. That's already a lot of vampire at the table. It's kinda spooky for some players. Maybe Rahadin. More on that later.

Your players might assume the food is poisoned. Let them cast whatever detection they want over it. It ends up being completely real, and that lands harder than poison would. The insult is the refusal.

And notice what the paranoia costs them. They burned a spell and a chunk of the evening proving the soup was soup. Hold that thought until the Ireena section, because that's the same hour Rahadin isn't at the table. He's doing some "errands". You don't mention it, though.

Somewhere in the middle of all this, Cyrus is serving. At some point he leans in and whispers a joke to one of the party. If the player cracks up or smirks at the table, Strahd acknowledges it. Nothing happens. Nothing changes. It's just a fun way to rock the table back to the light side before you come in with heavy Strahd again. A little bit of comic relief and then we ratchet up tension again. A lot of the "bad" jokes are from Part One.

9. IREENA A.K.A the mistake "smart" parties make

If Ireena is traveling with the party and hasn't been settled somewhere safe yet, she attends. I play her quiet. Shortest answers possible, doing her best to stay removed, wanting the evening over. She's there because she believes the party can protect her. If tempers start to flare, she's actually the one trying to keep them down.

Strahd's behavior toward her keys off the party. If she's been hurt in their care, he notices the wounds and berates them for it. Then the pitch, gentle and reasonable:

"I know you want to go home. I would like that for you as well. I only ask that these people keep you whole until you do."

And then, to the table, not to her:

"Eventually, she'll warm up to me. They always do."

He's coveting her, openly, and right now he's willing to wait this moment out. If they've hidden her or whisked her away somewhere, he comments on that too. He knows.

Big flashing red warning: these are fairly mature themes that we're dealing with here and you should, as a DM worth your salt, make sure that you and all of your players are comfortable RPing in this space. This is just how I play him and not everyone might want to play him this way. Unfortunately, if I mirror some of the worst of real life, it makes him very believable at the table.

Watch your pronouns with her, though, because this is where you can do the most emotional damage with the least effort. He does not talk about Ireena the way you talk about a person. He talks about her the way you talk about a painting somebody else is currently holding. She is his. That's not a declaration of love, it's a statement of ownership, said flat, as settled fact, in front of her. And when she's frightened or hurt or hardened, that's the party's doing. They dragged her through a swamp. They let her get cut. They've put ideas in her head. He is disappointed in the condition they've returned his "property" in. Don't use the word "property." It's a mindset for framing yourself as him.

Say it that plainly and you will hear your table's chairs move as people shift. It's the single most emotional thing he does all night. A.K.A Narrative leverage. We should be making the party feel some sort of way right now. And if you are, you're doing a good job.

It's also the point where I'd send you down to #7 before you use it. Ireena can take this because Ireena isn't a person at your table. The second you aim these lines at somebody's character and by proxy, attack them, the rules change. I mean that literally. Make sure everyone is comfortable in this space. Don't be a jerk.

Now here's the part I want to push on, because most "smart" groups leave Ireena home. They figure bringing her to Ravenloft is playing right into his hands. And they could be right. But it's actually the opposite. When she's alone is when she's most vulnerable. Even far away, tucked somewhere out of sight. :3

Remember the rule from the door, back in part 1. If Ireena isn't at that table, Rahadin isn't either, and now the players know exactly where he was all night. You can take her during the dinner. Or, crueler, imho the better option: have Rahadin just VISIT her, and let the party come home to find out what almost happened while they were eating salad and scanning the soup for poison. Nothing happened. That's the message. Nothing happened because nothing needed to. YET. Again you decide how your Strahd plays this point.

10. WHEN THEY GET FROGGY A.K.A allude, don't strike...until AFTER

Players will get froggy. Mine always do. Froggy within reason is fine. Nobody has ever actually swung at Strahd at my dinners, and if yours are polite-ish, everything above just plays out.

If they push past charming, don't attack them for it. A fight at the dinner table is a bad idea for everybody, yours truly included. Allude, don't strike. Save it for after. Just mark down who is annoying him and make sure, when the time comes to pay, that back twofold. :3

But have the procedure ready for the day somebody swings. What I'd do:

He does not swing back. He has a loadout for this and he prepared it before anybody sat down, we should be feeling comfortable on our home turf.

Wall of force. The room is now two rooms, he is on the other side of a pane of nothing with his wine still in his hand, and he says something withering about decorum and about how disappointing it is to have to say it out loud, at his own table, to guests. Then eventually he drops the wall and asks whether they'd like the next course.

If it's one specific loudmouth rather than the whole table, Otiluke's resilient sphere instead. He sits them in a bubble. Take a time-out like a child. Bonus points if you have Cyrus roll him out of dinner for a while.

(Neither spell is printed on his stat block, but both fit slots he actually has, and he has the run of the Amber Temple's library. I let him prepare like the wizard he is, same as I let him swap in shield and misty step when he's expecting a real fight.)

Why not just hit them? Because a hit back turns dinner into a boss fight five levels early, and absorbing the hit turns it into a lesson. He isn't showing them he can kill them. They already assume that. He's showing them he doesn't need to right now.

That's one free swing. If they keep going, the night is over and you switch modes hard, all at once, in the same breath: Strahd and his consorts come apart into mist, every light in the castle goes out, and the hunt starts. No speech, no countdown, no second warning. They are now loose in Ravenloft in the dark with a vampire who was polite to them ten seconds ago, and everything they walked past on the way in is still exactly where it was. At least that's my style. It might not be yours. :D

And once he's mist, understand what that actually means in a stone building: there is no room in that castle they can close him out of. A gap under a door is a door. If your players barricade themselves somewhere and feel clever about it, let them feel clever for about a minute.

Both outcomes are good. That's why you can afford to be generous at the table.

Because after dinner, they get to explore. And if they earned a lesson, you load up something with a little more CR and let it find them after the dinner. Strahd's just gonna pull a little beastie out of the crypt.

11. THE ROAM A.K.A Kevin McAllister's other, other house

How does the night actually flow? Dinner, the long conversation, all of the above. Afterward he walks them around the castle for a bit. Then he takes his leave. Business to attend to. They're free to stay the night if they're not too scared to. Or leave whenever they want. "The castle doesn't receive many visitors. Be careful going too far up or too far down because those even less so."

Players sneak off all the time. That's just a thing. Let them. We want them to. To quote one of my favorite movies, "snoop on them while they snoop on us." The castle itself is a far better surveillance tool than the players just walking around blind.

Why am I comfortable letting a party wander a vampire's castle unsupervised? Kevin McAllister expects the bad guys to get into the house eventually and sets up accordingly. He doesn't put all his traps on the outside. He puts them on the inside too. Strahd's castle is very, very layered, and if they push too hard, the castle pushes back all by itself.

  • The dragon statues you spent all that time describing on the way in. If they don't set them off tonight, they will go off at the wedding.
  • The gargoyles, all eight, same deal.
  • The chapel. There is a little statue in there that my players seem to steal a LOT. Let them. It's a useful thing that can happen tonight, because now you know exactly what kind of party this is. Strahd notices that missing.

The wandering IS the test, and it feeds the next act. We see what they poke at, what they pocket, what they try to steal (mine always lift something, usually the statue in the chapel). How cheeky they get and how deep they push up or down.

And there's one more thing loose in the castle with them that night, which is where I'd put Pidlwick II. He pays off three months later, and he's worth a writeup of his own. Maybe I'll consider it. ;p

One more tip: send the dinner EARLY-ish in the campaign, not late. I almost don't see the sense in doing it later. If you want another castle meeting down the road, send a second invitation. This first one is for letting them roam. The castle will remember them next time.

And that's the real reason to run it early and run it full. For most parties this is the first of two visits, and everybody they shake hands with tonight is standing somewhere in the second one. Rahadin. Escher. The four consorts. Cyrus. The thing in the hallway. The statue they pocketed and the room they weren't supposed to be in. You are not decorating a dinner. You're casting a wedding as well. If you do this right that whole scene is already warm when you get there. There's history. There's familiarity. And you're obviously planting seeds for fruit later.

12. THE RIDE HOME A.K.A "total freedom", supervised >.>

When Strahd leaves, the offer is simple: Cyrus will take them home whenever they please. Or anywhere else they'd like to go. No problem.

Think about what that offer actually is. They can go wherever they want. But we're bringing them there. If they take the carriage, he knows exactly where they went next. If they politely decline, fine, he was going to scry them anyway, so they might as well have taken the free trip. That slows them down considerably. This is actually even better. Pride comes before the fall. ;p

There is no version of this where he loses. Again, this is a toxic mechanic. Total freedom, provided and supervised by the person you're trying to get away from. Dropped off, picked up, and he's already waiting at the end of the shift. Control. Having him show a little bit of his upper hand for the moment. It reads creepy because it is. Look no further than how people act in real life.

And if you want the knife in it, put an expiry on the safe passage and don't tell them what it is.

Mine runs out the instant the last character steps down out of the carriage. Not when they get in. Not at the gate. The last boot hits the road, the driver is already pulling away, and whatever I've decided is waiting for them comes out of the treeline. Spawn. Dire wolves. Whatever their level has earned. We've already been calculating how big of a slap they're gonna need here. They will still have the good silverware taste in their mouths. :D

The reason this lands and doesn't feel cheap is that he kept his word. Exactly. To the letter, and not one inch past it. He offered them safe passage home and they got safe passage home, and the carriage did not stop and it did not stay. Nobody at that table thought to ask how long the offer was good for. Now they will ask, every single time, for the rest of the campaign, and that is a permanent change to how your players hear a promise from him. Should get the move and getting of the rest of the artifacts pretty quickly too.

13. WHAT HE'S DOING WHILE THEY RIDE HOME A.K.A villains have calendars

He didn't skip the fight tonight out of graciousness. He had somewhere to be. We have stuff to do. My villains have calendars and stuff happens on those calendar dates. They make active moves. We don't just watch the players. We advance our plots.

Here's the timeline, because the payoff depends on the players being able to do the math at some point. He excuses himself between the last course and the roam. They wander the castle, maybe sleep there, maybe not. They take the carriage home, and whatever's waiting at the drop-off is waiting at the drop-off. They sleep. Two days later somebody rides in with the news. Or they ride back to see it themselves.

If the party's been leaning on the wereravens and hasn't hidden it well, the news is that the tavern is gone and the Keepers are scattered into little cells all over Barovia. He went from pouring their wine to burning their friends' home in the same night. When your players line that up themselves, it plays REALLY well. That's a believable villain. I've never seen this not hit. You see the duality of him.

That's one example. He'll weaponize whatever he knows, and it won't necessarily be anything they gave him at dinner. It's the research he'd been doing for weeks. If you're unfamiliar with how to do this, just go back through some of my old Strahd posts. It's all pretty much covered there.

Then we wait and see what they do. We can punish accordingly later. Oh, and we will. :3

14. THE PRIVATE OFFER A.K.A collecting baseball cards

Last thing, and it's the trickiest play of the night. If Strahd is courting someone at that table, a successor candidate or a woman he's fixated on, the offer is never made in front of everyone. It happens in private. At the castle if he can isolate them for a moment, and if he can't, he finds another time. During that character's watch, some night soon. The isolation has to be real. This is part of his overall tactics, at least as I play him.

Who does he pick? Whoever has the darkest heart at that table, and I mean that as an actual evaluation and not a vibe. He is not looking for the meanest one. He's looking for the one who already believes that hard things have to be done and that they're the one who can be trusted to do them. The paladin who's been justifying a few of those things lately is a much better candidate than the rogue who steals from strangers, and if you've been paying attention all campaign you already know which of your players that is. Anyone that can kind of lean towards lawful evil tendencies or even chaotic neutral tendencies.

And there are two seats open, not one. The throne is the offer he makes out loud. The other one is Escher's, and Escher is still sitting in it. If you ran him upstairs in the guest room, the party has already met the man they'd be replacing, been charmed by him, maybe felt a little sorry for him. They just don't know yet that they were being shown the job's previous occupant. I love this scene because it just adds awkwardness even more to the table that you layer on with that awkwardness from Ireena.

The successor offer is genuinely good, which is what makes it dangerous:

"Rule in my stead. You don't even have to be a vampire if you don't want to. You rule until you're old and going to die, and when you die, I'll consider that my time to come back from vacation. Unless you'd like to stay. If you want the bite, you can take the bite."

The courtship offer is the same shape. Money, power, forever, the bite optional. And he'll even promise to let her friends go if she stays behind. Early on, before any of that, he just wants to talk. What's the party doing, what are they thinking, where are they heading. He's an abuser. Abuse and use. He wants to collect them like baseball cards.

BIG FLASHING NOTE AGAIN: if you're going to run the courtship on an actual player character, clear it with your players first. Out of game. You're doing a bit of roleplay, you're playing a character, and everyone at the table needs to understand that and be okay with it. Check boundaries before you make anybody feel the way Ireena feels. That's the whole trick, done safely: the player should squirm, not the person. This module already has above-average mature themes. If your table is comfortable playing in that space, it opens up a wealth of interesting role play. I say things in character I would never say otherwise. In that moment I'm not me. I'm somebody else and my players understand that. This is not permission to be weird at people. Or get yourself put on D&D Horror Stories!

15. AND THE ONE TIME IT WENT THE OTHER WAY A.K.A "Goldpetal" the double agent

Here's my favorite thing that ever came out of a dinner.

One campaign, a female PC, elf monk, let Strahd court her, and played him back as a double agent for a very long time. She gave him just enough that he trusted her, while she actively worked with the party the whole way. Everything she did was plausible. She worked both directions. The party knew just enough to trust her but she kept them in the dark enough that she could keep herself in deep cover as far as Strahd was concerned.

It went so deep that during the final fight, Strahd phased through the wall into a room he'd isolated her in, mid-battle, to give her instructions. He still had no reason not to trust her. He was waiting for her to execute a betrayal.

She ignored every word and helped kill him in the end. She took dark gifts though and ended up getting stuck there in Barovia. See my Shades of Grey ending post if you want more on that. She did keep her word about executing the betrayal, just not the one he had in mind. So all things considered, her ending is tragic but also remarkable at the same time. Iconic for me because she was the only one at any of my tables that was in Strahd's pocket enough to essentially understand all of his moves or most of them. And then prove herself useful enough to Strahd every time but also stay loyal to the party. Snoop on them while they snoop on us.

He spent the whole campaign running cons on the party. The armor bit, Vasili, etc. She figured out how to run one right back at him, my only table to do so. She also played the part that would make a female Bond villain blush. If your table produces a moment like that, that's the one they'll bring up every time you see them. I would like to hear about it.

THE FEAST LOADOUT

The seventh loadout, for the one night he's hosting. Slots untouched, 4/3/3/3/1, fourteen prepared. ⭐ means it's printed on the legacy block, so there's no argument to have about it. The rest are library swaps, same as the other six.

1st (4 slots)

  • ⭐ Comprehend languages. The fourth bottle. He can read the label. That's why he pours it for the one person at the table who also can.
  • ⭐ Sleep. The non-lethal answer if somebody swings. He'd rather they woke up embarrassed than dead.
  • Shield.

2nd (3 slots), and this is the whole loadout

  • ⭐ Detect thoughts, x3. Three slots, one minute each, one target at a time. That's three separate reads across a four-course dinner, call it one per course. Surface thoughts need no save. He only rolls if he digs, and if he digs and they make the save, the spell ends and they feel it. This is the engine under every "how does he know that" moment in the post.
  • ⭐ Mirror image.

3rd (3 slots)

  • Counterspell. Not to win. To be seen doing it.
  • Dispel magic.
  • ⭐ Nondetection, on himself. Every divination they sneak at the host over dinner comes back clean. Same trick as Nystul's on the armor.

4th (3 slots)

  • Otiluke's resilient sphere. The hamster ball. One loudmouth, removed from the conversation, nobody hurt.
  • ⭐ Greater invisibility.
  • Mordenkainen's private sanctum, cast on the hall beforehand. Nobody scries this room afterward. A four hundred year old paranoid counter-scries his own dining room.

5th (1 slot)

  • Wall of force. That's the call, and notice what he gave up for it. No scrying, he did that weeks ago. No dominate person. No hold monster. He spent his one big slot on splitting a room in half without hurting anybody.

Off the sheet, and this is the part that matters:

  • Charm. DC 17 Wisdom, twenty four hours, and no immune-on-a-success clause, so he can just keep trying, person after person, all night. The best weapon at that table costs him no slot at all.
  • Deathly Choir. Rahadin, seated. Rahadin is a prepared spell Strahd didn't have to prepare.
  • Lair actions, initiative count 20, one per round. Pass through walls, doors, ceilings and floors until initiative 20 next round. Or open and close every door and window and magically lock them, DC 20 Strength to force. Or summon the angry spirit of somebody who died in the castle to strike one hostile creature. He only gets one of the three each round, which is a real decision six times over if it goes loud.
  • Misty Escape under everything, Regeneration on top of it, and mist form in a stone building where a gap under a door is a door.
  • Three consorts and Escher, already seated. He didn't cast those either.

What got cut from the standard Social loadout: dimension door, the second prepped exit, Beucephalus on ethereal standby. All of it. He's home. The slots that would have bought his way out bought detect thoughts instead.

The tell: every other loadout announces itself. This one's tell is the absence of one. He's seated before they walk in, he never stands, and nobody sees him cast anything all night.

THE MENU

Four courses. Read it out slowly.

First: chilled beetroot soup, soured cream, dill. Served very cold, and very red.

Second: trout from Lake Zarovich, poached in white wine, brown butter, pickled ramps. The only Barovian dish on the table. Everything else came through the mists.

Third: a saddle of venison roasted rare, black cherries and juniper, potato dumplings, and winter greens that have no business existing in this valley.

Fourth: poppyseed roll and honeyed apricots, figs and grapes on the board. Fruit nobody at that table has seen in living memory.

The wine, offered as a choice: Red Dragon Crush (the good one, and he'll say so), Champagne du le Stomp, Purple Grapemash No. 3. And a fourth bottle whose label somebody at the table can read, in a language that has no business being in this valley either. I use Drow Elf Mushroom Wine. Poured last, for whoever he's most interested in so far.

Everything is completely real. Let them cast whatever they want over it.

PIDLWICK II

Why I start him at the dinner instead of saving him for the toy room.

Start small, during the meal. Something moves at the end of a hallway. A door that was open is closed. One of the little wooden things on a shelf is facing a different way than it was.

Then once they're loose in the castle, let him shadow them. He's a clockwork jester, he's a foot and a half of malice with a dart, and he's going to do cheeky things. Untie a pack. Take one specific item and leave the rest. Follow them one room behind for twenty minutes and let a passive Perception finally catch him standing perfectly still where a toy has no business standing.

The party might find him. That's the good outcome. Let them.

Two reasons he earns the screen time on dinner night. First, everything else in the post is about being watched by something with an agenda, and he's the version of that with no agenda at all, which is worse in a different way. Second, he pays off much later: if he ends up traveling with the party and they get the ghost of the original Pidlwick to show up, the ghost will look right at the clockwork one and tell them how he actually died.

That's a hell of a thing to have set up three months earlier at a dinner party.

TWO RULINGS THAT DIDN'T FIT IN THE POST

Charm, at the dinner table. Don't charm a player if your players hate losing control of their characters, and most do. Charm an NPC instead, ideally one they like. The party watches somebody they trust turn pleasant and agreeable and slightly wrong, and they learn exactly what the ability does without anybody at the table losing their turn to it.

If you do charm a PC, ask for something small and awful rather than something violent. Hand me your other knife, my friend. Pour for the lady. Sit closer. Nobody gets hurt and everybody understands.

The Vistani curse, on the way out. If he's feeling nasty. House rule and I'll own it: that's a Vistani feature, not his, and I let him use it anyway. Have the save rolled after they leave. After they rest for the first time. They will not connect it to the dinner right away, and the "dinner" keeps happening to them for weeks.
