**Draft v0.1** for red-team review. Continues directly from **107** at the end of First Watch.

## THE BELL AT THE WRONG HOUR

---

### 200

The bell does not stop.

The rhythm of a dusk bell is one stroke, a pause the length of a breath, another, unhurried, like a man counting. This is not that. This is a rope being hauled back and forth violently by somebody who has stopped counting, strokes falling over each other, the clapper hitting on the backswing. It is the sound of a bell being rung by a man who has decided the only thing left that matters is that it be loud.

All over Brackenford, shutters that closed an hour ago are opening again. Faces in gaps. The village listening to its own alarm and not knowing what it means, because the bell has never rung an alarm before tonight.

→ **201**

---

### 201

Hett Vance takes about two seconds.

You watch him work it out: one road, one ford, one bell, one recruit, and four places that all need somebody right now.

"Right," he says. "I'm going up the hill. Doran went up there with the box and he hasn't come down and if the toll wasn't paid tonight then none of the rest of this matters."

"And me?"

"You're the watch." He is already turning. "The village is yours. I'm sorry. I'd give you six men if I had them."

*If you have **VOUCHED**:* you have already learned tonight that this village runs on people quietly carrying things for it, and you are looking at the man who carries the most, and who is about to go up that hill alone.

→ "Then tell me the fourth rule." **202**
→ "What do I do about the bell?" **203**
→ Let him go. **205**

---

### 202

He stops.

"The fourth rule," Hett says, "is that you will want to be the one who fixes it. Every man I ever put in that tabard wanted that. It's why they take the job."

The bell hammers on behind him.

"And the job isn't that. The job is you're a light that moves on a schedule, so a village full of people asleep behind doors knows the night's still got somebody in it. That's all. You don't beat the dark, watchman. You just refuse to leave it on its own." He looks at you a moment longer. "Now: you'll break that rule tonight, because everyone does. Just know which rule it is you're breaking when you do."

→ **205**

---

### 203

"Anselm's rung that bell twice a day for a month without missing," Hett says. "If he's ringing it now, he's got a reason, and the reason is not that he wants company."

"Or somebody else is on the rope."

Hett stops.

"Yes," he says slowly. "Or that."

He does not have an answer for it. That is somehow the worst part of the night so far: the captain of the militia standing in the frost with his mouth open, out of answers, in front of the new hire.

→ "Then tell me the fourth rule." **202**
→ Let him go. **205**

### 205

He goes up the hill road at a run with his lantern swinging, and you stand at the bottom of a village that is waking up frightened, and you are the watch.

Then, out on the ice, something moves.

→ **206**

---

### 206

The ford is a grey floor from bank to bank and there is a man walking across it.

He is coming from the far side, from the Drowned Lane, unhurried, on two legs, in the middle of the crossing where the water used to run deepest. He has no lantern. He is not carrying anything. His arms swing.

He is walking like a man who has walked this crossing ten thousand times, which is the thing that stops your breath, because everyone in Brackenford walks the ford in the same careful hitching way, feeling for the flat stones with their feet, and he is not doing that, because there is nothing under him but ice and he knows it.

*If you have darkvision:* the lantern is doing nothing for you that your own eyes are not. Out past its throw you can see him whole, in grey, and his face is pleasant, and he is not looking at the ice or the far bank or the village lights. He has been looking at you since he stepped onto the crossing.

→ Raise the lantern and challenge him. **207**
→ Get off the bank and watch from cover. **208**
→ Run for the village ahead of him. **210**

---

### 207

"Stand where you are!"

Your voice goes out across the ice and comes back off the far bank, and it sounds young.

The man stops.

He stands in the middle of the frozen river with his back to the moor and looks at you across forty feet of grey, and your lantern reaches him, just, at the far end of its throw.

*If you were trained to read a fight before it starts:* he stopped the instant you told him to, and he stopped at forty feet, which is the distance a man picks when he wants to be looked at. His hands are empty and open and well away from his sides. He is standing on wet ice in the dead of winter and nothing in his weight is braced against it.

He is a shepherd, forty-odd, a heavy coat, a beard going grey at the chin. There is frost in it. There is frost on his eyelashes and in the seams of his coat and packed white in the folds of his ears, the way frost forms on a thing that has been out all night and is the same temperature as the air.

He says, "Evening." His breath does not show.

→ **211**

### 208

You get down behind the overturned chair on the bank and put the lantern behind you, and you watch him come.

He walks up out of the ford and onto the near bank six feet from where you are crouched, and he does not look at you, and you are close enough to hear him.

He is humming. Softly, tunelessly, the way a man does walking home late.

There is frost packed white in the seams of his coat and in his beard, and it is not melting, and he passes you and goes up the slope toward the village and the lights.

He is going to the top of the square. To the inn.

### 211

You know who it is before you let yourself know it.

There is a woman at the top of this village who has set a clean mug in front of an empty chair every dusk for a year, in front of forty people, without breaking conversation, because she is not grieving at all. She is waiting. Once, on a night the ford froze, she heard him at her door.

Tobin Tullamore has come home.

*If you have **MUGSET**:* she told you herself, with both hands flat on the bar, in the voice she uses for arguments about sheep, and you did not believe her, and you were polite about not believing her, and she saw that too.

## THE DEAD HOURS

---

### 220

**The dead hours.**

It is somewhere past midnight. The bell is still going. There is a man walking up your village who has been dead since last autumn, and he is going to knock on a door that will be opened.

And there are four places in Brackenford that need the watch right now, and one of you.

*Choose where you go. When you return here, choose again, somewhere you have not yet been: the night does not offer the same crisis twice.*

*If Nell is with you:* she looks down each of the four roads in turn, and then up at you, and waits. She has no advice. She is just not leaving.

## THE BROKEN WHEEL

---

### 230

You come up into the square at a run and the inn is the only building in Brackenford with its shutters still open.

Of course it is. Mara Tullamore called last orders two hours early tonight and put forty people out into the cold and barred nothing, because she has spent a year keeping a door that a man might come back to.

There is a light in the window. There is a clean mug on a table in front of an empty chair by a fire that has been kept up.

And there is a knock.

→ **231**

---

### 231

Three knocks. Not loud. The knock of a man who lives there and has lost his key.

Then, through the door, in a voice with a smile in it:

"Mara. It's cold out here, love."

Inside, something falls over. You hear her cross the room. You hear her stop.

You are twenty feet away in the square with a lantern and a tabard and about four seconds.

→ Get between him and the door. **232**
→ Shout to warn her. **236**
→ Go around the back and get in first. **238**

---

### 232

You put yourself on the step between Tobin Tullamore and his own front door, and up close, in lantern light, he is worse.

He is not rotten. Nothing about him is rotten. He is a healthy shepherd of forty-odd with frost in his beard and colour in his cheeks and every single thing right about him except that he is not making breath in the cold, and his eyes track a half-beat late, the way a man's do when he is listening to a translation.

"You're new," he says pleasantly. "Hett get you? Good lad, Hett. Would you mind? My wife's just inside."

*If you can turn undead:* you do not have to raise the symbol to know. That cold pull you were trained to feel across a room, the one that tells you where the dead are standing before you turn your head, is not on this step at all. Whatever is in front of you does not answer to it, and nothing you were taught covers a thing that does not answer.

→ "You've been gone a year." **233**
→ "What's your wife's name?" **234**
→ Say nothing. Stand there. **235**

---

### 233

"I have," Tobin agrees. "It's been a long one. I've walked a long way tonight, son, and I'd like to sit down."

And he says it so exactly right, so tiredly and so warmly and so much like a man at the end of a hard road, that you feel the pull of it in your chest: the enormous, decent, human urge to step aside, because you are twenty-something and standing between a shepherd and his wife.

*Make a DC 14 Wisdom saving throw.*

**Success.** You do not move. → **235**
**Failure.** You take half a step to the side before you catch yourself, and he is already moving, and your shoulder is against the door frame with your arm across it and it is nearly not enough. **Tick one box on the Dawn Track.** → **235**

### 234

"What's your wife's name?"

Tobin blinks. "Mara," he says. "Mara Tullamore. Ran the Wheel since her father. Sets a mug out."

It is right. All of it is right, and he answers it easily, and it is the easiness that undoes you, because a thing that had to think about it would be less frightening than a thing that does not.

Then, from the other side of the door, close, barely above a breath, at the height of a woman's mouth against the wood:

"Tobin. Say the other thing."

→ **235**

---

### 235

The door does not open.

There is a long silence on that step, the three of you, and the bell hammering away across the square, and then Mara Tullamore says, through her own front door, in a voice so level it takes you a moment to hear what it costs:

"You've not asked after the dog."

Nothing on the step moves.

"He'd have asked after the dog," she says. "First thing, before me, every time, thirty years. Where's the dog." A breath. "You've not asked."

And the thing on the doorstep stops smiling. Not angrily. It simply stops, the way a candle flame stops when it is snuffed, all at once and completely.

→ **240**

---

### 236

You shout her name across the square.

The door opens.

It opens because a woman who has waited a year hears a shout outside and thinks *something is happening to him*, and she has her hand on the latch before the thought finishes, and this is not stupidity, this is what a year of hoping does to a person.

Mara Tullamore stands in her own doorway in the light with her husband in front of her.

**Tick one box on the Dawn Track.**

→ **237**

---

### 237

For a second nobody does anything.

Mara looks at him. He looks at her. He says, "Hello, love," and it is his voice, it is absolutely his voice, and you watch a year come apart in a woman's face.

And then she looks at his beard, at the frost packed white in it, at the air in front of his mouth where his breath is not.

She is a woman who has run a room of forty drunk men for thirty years, and you will never in your life forget how fast she moves. The door slams. The bar comes down. It happens in less than a second and it happens with her husband's hand on the frame, and the door catches his fingers, and he does not react to that at all.

→ **240**

---

### 238

You go around the back through the yard, over the wall, in through the scullery where Mara has left the latch off the way she has left it off every night for a year.

You come out into the common room behind her.

She is standing at the door with her hand on the bar and her forehead against the wood and she is listening to her husband ask to be let in, and she has been standing like that for some time.

She does not turn around. She says, "Don't."

→ "Don't what?" **239**
→ Say nothing. **239**

---

### 239

"Don't tell me," Mara says. "I know. I've known since the second knock. He always did four."

Her hand is still on the bar. She has not moved it and she has not lifted it.

"A year I've kept that mug." Her voice does not break, and that is the thing you will carry. "And it came, and it's not him, and I have to be the one that keeps the door. Not you. Me. Or I'll spend the rest of my life wondering." She finally looks at you. "So stand there and let me do it."

You stand there.

Outside, a voice with a smile in it says her name twice more, and then stops.

→ **240**

---

### 240

The thing that is not Tobin Tullamore stands on the step for a while.

Then it walks away, unhurried, back down the square toward the ford, and it does not look at the windows it passes, and the frost on the cobbles does not squeak under it.

*If you can cast detect magic:* you get it off before the thing reaches the corner, and the reading is worse than any reading would have been. There is nothing on it. No school, no residue, no working of any kind laid on a shepherd who has been dead since autumn. Nobody cast this.

Inside, Mara sits down on the floor with her back against the door she has just kept, and she does not cry, and she says, "Get out. Please. I'll be all right in the morning and I won't be able to look at you for a month."

Write the codeword **DOORHELD**.

**Tick one box on the Dawn Track.**

→ **241**

### 241

On the table by the fire, the clean mug is still there in front of the empty chair.

Halfway to the door you stop, because you have understood something and it is too late to unknow it.

It knew to come here. Of everything in this village, of every door with a light behind it, it came up the whole length of the square to the one door in Brackenford that was going to open.

Something has been watching how this village grieves.


→ Back to the dead hours. **220**

## THE SHRINE OF THE QUIET HAND

---

### 250

You go in with the lamp up, because whatever is on that rope, you want to see it. **The lamp drinks a unit: mark off one oil.**

The noise inside the shrine is enormous.

You have heard this bell from across the village every dusk of your life and you have never heard it from six feet away, and it is not a sound anymore, it is a force you feel in your chest and in your teeth. The stone is shaking. The frozen leaves piled against the door are dancing on the step.

The door has not opened in a month.

→ Knock. **251**
→ Break it down. **253**
→ Try the door. **252**

---

### 251

You hammer on it with the side of your fist and you get nothing, because nothing could be heard in there, because there is a bell going off inside a stone box.

You are going to have to open it.

→ Break it down. **253**
→ Try the door. **252**

---

### 252

You try the latch first, because you are the sort of person who tries the latch first, and this is the second time tonight that being that sort of person has been worth something.

It is not locked.

It has not been locked all month. A man sat in a chair behind an unlocked door for thirty days and the entire village agreed, without ever discussing it, that the door was shut. You think about the leaves frozen against it. Nobody even tried.


→ Go in. **254**

---

### 253

*Make a DC 13 Strength (Athletics) check. On Dark Watch, the door is old oak swollen into a frozen frame: DC 15.*

**Success.** The door goes in on the second shoulder and you go with it, into the noise. → **254**

**Failure.** You put your shoulder into a door that opens outward, twice, before you work out what is wrong with the situation. Take 1d4 bludgeoning damage, feel exactly as clever as you deserve, and **tick one box on the Dawn Track**. → **252**

### 254

Inside is a forest of dead candles and a wall of sound.

Brother Anselm is on the rope. He is a gaunt man in a robe that has not been off him in a month and he is hauling with both hands and the whole of his weight, over and over, badly, out of time, and he has been doing it long enough that his palms have opened up and there is blood on the rope in a band the width of a hand.

He does not hear you. He does not hear anything. He is thirty days without sleep and long past the end of his strength, and he is ringing.

→ Take the rope from him. **255**
→ Get in front of him where he can see you. **256**

---

### 255

You get a hand on the rope above his and take the weight, and for a moment he fights you for it, with surprising strength, the way a drowning man fights.

Then he sees the tabard.

Anselm lets go and steps back and stands swaying in the middle of his own shrine with his hands open in front of him, palms up, red, and looks at them as though they belong to somebody he used to know.

The bell runs down. It takes a long time. The last few strokes come slower and slower and the silence that arrives afterward is the loudest thing that has happened tonight.

→ **257**

---

### 256

You get in front of him and he looks straight through you for three full strokes before you resolve into a person.

"You," he says. He does not stop hauling. "The watch. Good. Good."

"What are you doing?"

"Waking them." Haul. "Every soul in this village. Waking them." Haul. "Because I have rung this bell on its exact schedule for thirty days, believing the schedule itself was the lock that kept the thing on the hill out, and I have just broken it on purpose, and now they will all know something is wrong, which is the only thing I have left that is worth anything."

→ Take the rope from him. **255**

---

### 257

In the silence Anselm sits down on the step of his own altar, which is the first time he has sat anywhere but that chair in a month.

"It crossed the ford," he says. "I watched it come down off the hill and across the ice. I have been watching that hill through a shutter for thirty days and tonight something walked off it."

He looks at his hands.

*If you are proficient in Religion:* you know the rite he means. The dusk bell of the Quiet Hand is a courtesy to the dead and a call to the living, and in every liturgy you have ever read that is the whole of it. There is no ward in a schedule. He built that lock himself, out of nothing, in a room with the door unlocked, and it may have held anyway.

"So I rang. At midnight. Which I have never done." A small dry sound that might be a laugh. "There. It is done. Thirty days of a lock I could not test, and I have broken it myself, on purpose, to make a noise."

→ "You did the right thing." **258**
→ "Was it the lock?" **259**
→ "Can you stand?" **260**

---

### 258

"I do not know that," Anselm says. "That is what I am telling you. I have never known that."

He looks up, and for the first time there is something in his face other than exhaustion.

"But a man came to my door tonight in a tabard and asked if I was all right, and nobody had asked, and I find that I have been thinking about it for four hours." He works his ruined hands open and closed. "So perhaps. Perhaps I did."

→ "Can you stand?" **260**

---

### 259

"Ask me at dawn," he says.

"That's not an answer."

"No. It is the only honest one available and I have spent a month learning to live inside it." He tips his head back against the altar. "Everything I have done since the harvest has been done on a belief I cannot test. So has everything Sefa does with her crusts on the stone. So has everything Goodwife Alder decided in her kitchen. Three of us, watchman, holding this village up with three different guesses, none of us speaking to the others, and it has *worked*, and not one of us knows which part is the part that works."


→ "Can you stand?" **260**

---

### 260

He can, barely, and he does not want to.

*If you get him up and out, or if you make a DC 12 Wisdom (Medicine) check to see how far gone he actually is:* thirty days without sleep has done what thirty days without sleep does. He is not mad. He is starved and frozen and hallucinating from exhaustion and he needs a warm room and eight hours, and he will not have them, because he will not go.

"The bell is rung," Anselm says. "The village is awake. I have nothing else." Then, with his eyes already closing: "Take it."

"Take what?"

"The vigil." He is not being poetic. He is handing over a job. "It is yours. You are the one who is outside."

Write the codeword **RELIEVED**.

**Tick one box on the Dawn Track.**

→ **261**

### 261

The bell rope hangs in the dark with a band of blood on it at the height of a man's hands.

*If you have **HANDSIGN**:* he told you at dusk to get an answer before you needed it, and you did not, and now you are the one holding the rope.

## THE SQUARE

---

### 270

*Go and get the children.*

He said it at dusk, through a shut door, and you had no idea it was an instruction.

The square is not empty now. The bell has done what Anselm meant it to do: there are people in the doorways all around it, in coats over nightclothes, forty or fifty of them, looking at each other and at the shrine and at you, waiting for somebody to say the thing that turns a frightened crowd into a village doing something.

Every face turns to the tabard.

→ **271**

---

### 271

You have never spoken to fifty people in your life.

*Choose how you handle it. There is no wrong answer, but there is an honest one.*

→ Tell them the truth: the ford is frozen, something came across it, get inside. **272**
→ Tell them what to do and not why. **273**
→ Find Goodwife Alder and let her do it. **274**

---

### 272

You tell them, and it goes badly for about ten seconds.

A woman starts crying. A man at the back says *the ford's froze* in the particular voice of somebody repeating a thing to make it smaller, and it does not get smaller, it gets around the square in about four seconds.

Then a big man you do not know says, "Right, whose kids are out?" and the whole thing turns over, because the one thing a frightened village is genuinely excellent at is counting children.

**Tick one box on the Dawn Track.**

→ **275**

---

### 273

"Everyone inside. Bar the doors. Now."

It is the tabard that does it, not you. Fifty people in a cold square hear an instruction in a uniform and about forty of them start moving, and it is going well right up until a woman at the front says, "Where's Ada?"

And then it is not going well, because you have told them to get behind doors without telling them why, and now there is a child unaccounted for and no information in the square, and panic fills a vacuum faster than sense does.

**Tick two boxes on the Dawn Track.**

→ **275**

---

### 274

Goodwife Alder is already in the square.

She is a plump, gracious woman in a good coat over her nightdress and she is standing at the edge of the crowd being visibly nobody in particular, and when you go to her she looks at you with an expression you will not understand for another year.

"You'll want me to say it," she says.

"They'll listen to you."

"They will." She sighs, and it is the sigh of a woman who has been running this village from a kitchen table for a decade and would have liked, once, for it to have cost somebody else something. "Help me up onto the well, dear. Nobody can hear me from level ground."

She stands on the lip of the dry well and says eleven sentences and the square empties in under two minutes with every child accounted for.


→ **275**

---

### 275

And then somebody screams from the far side of the square, and the count goes wrong.

There is a child up on the wellhead. Six, maybe seven, in a nightshirt and somebody's boots, standing on the stone lip in the light of the lantern somebody left on the wellhead, and she is not screaming. Her mother is.

The child is looking down at the plank cover.

The plank cover is pushed aside about a hand's width, and it is moving.

→ **276**

---

### 276

You get about four steps before the first one comes out.

It comes up out of a dry well like something poured upward: long grey legs, a body the size of a wolfhound, wings that open with a sound like a sail filling. It makes no call. It lands on the wellhead beside the child, folds, and turns its head to look at the fifty people in the square with the flat interest of a thing deciding where to start.

The second one is already out and standing on the notice board.

*If you are proficient in Animal Handling:* neither of them is angry, and that is the part that frightens you. Wild things puff and bluster and warn you off first. These two are quiet and loose and unhurried, and the one on the wellhead has not once looked down at the child beside it, because it does not need to. It already knows where she is.

*If you have **LARDER**:* you know exactly what these are, and you know how they hunt: in pairs, one chasing the prey into a panic while the other waits where the running will end.

→ **277**

---

### 277

The square empties around you in every direction at once: fifty people running for fifty doors, and two grey shapes owning the air above them, and one child standing still on the wellhead in a circle of lantern light. You are the only armed thing in Brackenford moving toward the well instead of away from it.

This is a fight now. **Roll initiative.** Here is what you are fighting, and how it will behave.

**FEN-SHRIKE (two)**

Medium beast. AC 13. Speed 30 ft., fly 50 ft.
STR 12 (+1) DEX 16 (+3) CON 13 (+1) INT 3 (-4) WIS 14 (+2) CHA 5 (-3)

**Beak.** Melee attack, +5 to hit, reach 5 ft. Hit: 1d8+3 piercing damage.
**Silent Rush.** Advantage on its first attack roll against a creature that has not yet acted in combat.

**How the pair fights.** They hunt like a team that has done this before, and each has a job.
**The first shrike**, the one that landed on the notice board, is the chaser. On its turn it flies at the biggest cluster of running villagers to scatter them. It only attacks a person if nothing gets in its way.
**The second shrike**, the one on the wellhead, is the catcher. It stays beside the child and attacks the first thing that comes within ten feet of the well. It chases nobody. It is waiting for what the chaser drives toward it.
Round 1: if you go for the child, the catcher attacks you with Silent Rush. If you go for the chaser instead, the catcher grabs the child.
If either shrike drops to half its hit points or fewer, it screams, the first sound either has made, and both fly for the far bank together.
If you get the child off the wellhead and behind a door, both leave within two rounds. They did not come here to fight you. They came here to hunt.
**Getting her out:** the well is thirty feet from the nearest door. Reaching her takes your move; lifting her off the wellhead takes your action, and if the catcher is alive and within ten feet of the well, it gets one attack against you as you lift her. Carrying her, you move at half speed. Any door on that square opens for a child without being asked.

*If you fight with a shield:* as you lift her you can get the shield up over the both of you and take the catcher's attack on the boards instead. That attack is made with disadvantage. You carry her out one armed and half blind behind the rim, and your back will remember it for a week.

*If Nell is with you:* send her to the wellhead and she goes, flat out, and stands over the child. The catcher will not go near her. A dog standing its ground over a child is nothing it has ever hunted, and it does not know what to do with one, so while Nell guards the child, the shrike attacks Nell instead of taking her.

## THE HILL ROAD

### 290

Rule three: if you see something you don't understand, you do not chase it, you come and get me.

The man who gave you that rule went up this road forty minutes ago and has not come back, and there is nobody left to get.

### 291

Hett's, going up, long strides, running. Doran's, going up earlier, heavier, one boot dragging slightly the way a man walks when he is carrying something in both arms.

And a third set, coming down, over the top of both.

### 292

Halfway up, where the road bends around a shoulder of the hill and the whole village lays itself out below you in the dark, you find the strongbox.

It is sitting in the middle of the road, upright, closed, neatly, on a patch of frost that has been swept clear around it in a rough circle about a yard across.

It is not damaged. It has not been forced. It is simply sitting there in the road with the coins still in it, the way a thing is put down by somebody who has been handed it and found it did not interest them.

*If you are proficient in Investigation:* the swept circle around it is the wrong shape for wind and the wrong shape for a man with a broom. The frost was not brushed outward from the box. It was pushed back from something that stood where the box is standing.

*If you have **VOUCHED**:* Doran Welk has carried this box up this road on freezing nights for a year, because the year he refused, his eldest did not come home.

### 293

Coppers, mostly. A year of a village's crossings, counted and bagged in linen, and a few silver on the top, and a folded square of paper.

### 294

### 295

In this cold there is no way to tell how long he has been dead. But the ground under him is not thawed at all, and a living man sitting for even a minute melts the frost beneath him. Whatever happened to Doran happened the moment he sat down.

There is no wound on him. Nothing took anything from him. He walked up this road with a box of coppers to pay a toll he hated on behalf of a village that resented him for collecting it, and something met him on the way, and looked at him, and did not want the box.

*If you have a healing spell or a healer's kit:* you try anyway. You are young and it is Doran and your hands do it before you decide to. The kit finds nothing to dress and the spell goes out of you and comes back with nothing to mend, because nothing in this man is broken. He is whole. He is simply finished.

*If you have **VOUCHED**:* he asked you not to have seen him on this road tonight, and you agreed, and that is the last conversation he had with anyone.

## THE TURN

### 310

*Play this entry when your visits are used up.*

You come back into the square somewhere in the deepest part of the night, in that hour when the cold stops being weather and starts being a place you live, and you stop, because something is different and it takes you a moment to find it.

The bell is not ringing. That has been true for a while.

The village is dark. Every shutter on the square, closed. Every door barred. Fifty people who were standing out here in their nightclothes an hour ago are behind wood, and the only light in Brackenford is the lantern somebody left burning on the wellhead, and yours.

*If you have **SHELTERED**:* you did that. You stood in the middle of the square and got a village behind its doors, and they are inside, and you are not.

*Otherwise, the doors closed without you. Somebody in every house made the same decision tonight, one at a time, and the result is a shut village with one light moving in it, and the light is yours.*

The deep cold has made the lamp burn its oil fast all night. **The lamp drinks a unit: mark off one oil.**

*If your lamp is dark:* the only light left in Brackenford is the one somebody set on the wellhead, and you are carrying a dead lantern through a shut village.

### 311

*Count what the night has cost you and what you have.*

*If you do not have **DOORHELD**:* you were not there. You do not know what happened at the inn, and there is a light still on up there, and its shutters are open.

### 312

Out on the ice, the sound starts.

It is not loud. It is the sound a frozen pond makes when the weight comes off it in the morning, a long low complaining crack that travels, and it goes all the way across the Wend from the far bank to the near one and stops.

Then it comes again.

Something is walking back across the ford, and this time the ice is talking about it, which means this time it is heavier.

*If your passive Wisdom (Perception) is 14 or higher:* you catch the spacing. The cracks come evenly, at the pace of a man walking who has nowhere he needs to be. It has not slipped once on that ice, and it has not stopped once to listen for you.

### 313

"Aye." His teeth are chattering and he is trying to hide it. "So we'll want it dark."
